# 01 — Shot-group fault taxonomy

**Task:** `shot-group-taxonomy`. **Scope:** research only. Compiling what instructors
already teach. **Not written here:** any diagnosis of any shooter, or any coaching advice.

Every entry is a *hypothesis a pattern is consistent with*, never a verdict. The confidence
column records what the sources actually support — and where the literature gives no
statistics, it reads `not stated`. **No number in this file is invented.**

---

## 1. The rule this file follows

From the brief: *"Your trigger pull is jerking"* is a diagnosis presented as fact.
*"This pattern is consistent with X, and also with Y; Z is a common cause"* is a hypothesis.

**Every row below is a hypothesis.** The single most important entry in this file is §5:
a named, credentialed instructor argues that this entire taxonomy should not be used for
diagnosis at all.

## 2. Direction conventions

`dispersion.ts` measures shots in inches from the aim point, with `poi` = centre of impact.
"High" means `poi.y` up; "left"/"right" follow the **shooter's** frame. The canonical chart
states: *"The following groups assume a right handed, right eye dominant shooter. For
left-handed shooters, reverse the groups."* (Target Shooting Canada, 2003.) **A left-handed
or left-eye-dominant shooter must mirror every entry below.**

## 3. The patterns — what the chart actually says

The canonical chart (Target Shooting Canada, 2003; circulated widely, and the file the brief
names as "Georgia 4-H Pistol Group Analysis" is a **repost** of it) gives these causes
verbatim. Confidence is **my assessment of how strongly the direction implies the fault**,
not a figure the sources supply — so it reads `not stated` where sources are silent.

| Pattern | Causes the chart gives | My confidence in the direction→fault link |
|---|---|---|
| **Vertical — high centre** | 1. Heeling (pressure with heel of hand). 2. Breaking wrist up, pulling pistol up. 3. Poor sight alignment: front sight too high. | **Low.** Three unrelated mechanisms; a cant alone produces this. |
| **Vertical — low centre** | 1. Poor concentration. 2. Poor follow-through, lowering gun before shot is away. 3. Poor sight alignment: front sight centred but too low. | **Low.** "Poor concentration" is not observable in a group. |
| **Left** | 1. Increasing grip pressure while shot breaks, bending wrist left. 2. Relaxing elbow while shot breaks. | **Low.** Two mechanisms, neither specific. |
| **Right** | 1. Extra pressure with thumb. 2. Poor sight alignment: front sight alignment off to the right. | **Low.** |
| **Low-left** | 1. Pushing the trigger, moving it and gun left. 2. Poor sight alignment off to the left. 3. Extra pressure with fingertips and/or pinkie. | **Low.** |
| **Low-right** | 1. Snatching the trigger. 2. Slackening the wrist while the pistol fires. | **Low.** |
| **High-right** | 1. Slackening the wrist. 2. Heeling. 3. Anticipating recoil. | **Low.** |
| **Low-left (cant variant)** | 1. Jerking: excessive trigger pressure, pushing down and left. 2. **Canting the pistol to the left and dropping the barrel.** | **Low**, and note cause 2 alone can produce a *vertical* low group too. |
| **Horizontal centre placement** | 1. Poor stance → bad natural point of aim. 2. Poor handgrip → bad natural point of aim. 3. Poor stance → body sway right and left. | **Very low.** This is "several things at once". |
| **No group / scattered** | 1. "All problems are evident, nothing specific stands out." 2. Start with natural point of aim, then grip and trigger squeeze. | **Very low.** The chart itself declines to diagnose. |
## 4. Named faults (as distinct from pattern positions)

These are the mechanism names the sources use. Each can appear as a pattern position *or*
change group shape without shifting POI.

| Fault | Signature | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Thumbing** | Aegis: *"Too much thumb pressure that drives the barrel toward the side of the strong hand."* | Direction is fairly consistent, but **specificity is not** — see §5. |
| **Heeling** | Aegis: *"driving the heel of your hand in to the pistol grip."* Appears in both high-centre and high-right on the chart. | Low — one fault, two patterns. |
| **Trigger finger placement** | Aegis: *"usually too much or too little on the trigger which pushes the barrel inward or outward."* | Low — "too much" and "too little" both push, in different directions. |
| **Anticipation / riding recoil** | Aegis: *"Pushing on the gun prior to recoil... OR 'riding' the recoil by unconsciously 'assisting' the elevation of the muzzle."* | Low — this is a **timing** fault. Nothing in a static photo records when the hand moved. |
| **Jerking** | Aegis: *"Jerking the trigger via a steady increase in trigger pressure which causes unnecessary movement in the barrel."* Chart: down and left. | Low. |
| **Grip pressure** | Aegis: *"usually strong & support side & again too much & too little."* | Low. |
| **Canting** | Chart: *"Canting the pistol to the left and dropping the barrel."* | **Medium — the one fault a photo genuinely supports**, because cant is directly visible in the target's ellipse. Still ambiguous between cant angle and hand position. |
| **Poor sight alignment** | Chart lists it as a cause for high-centre, low-centre, right, and low-left. | Low — a **sight picture is not in a target photo at all.** See §6. |

## 5. The disagreement — and it is not subtle

**Patrick Henry, Aegis Academy** (instructor; author of Aegis training material), writing
about pistol correction charts in general — the exact artifact the brief names:

> "Pistol Correction Charts... are the probably the most misused tool in the training
> industry. They are popular because they superficially promise a quick 'one-size-fits-all'
> cure for common pistol shooting errors."

> "**Every shooter is unique**, with body mechanics, habits, and trends that combine in a way
> that is meaningful only to that shooter. **If you didn't make the pistol correction chart,
> that chart is not universally applicable to you.** It might be that error, or any other
> number of combinations of errors that is causing you to miss the center of the target."

> "There are certainly trends that can be identified, but they are designed to help diagnose
> **trends, not individual shots** and are far from universally applicable. **The best
> solution to a consistent error is a quality coach!**"

> "The pattern of bullet impacts can only provide evidence that **there is an error in
> shooting technique**. Without actually observing the technique in practice, it is impossible
> to accurately assess the fault and provide effective corrective actions. **It would be like
> looking at an image of golf ball impacts and trying to diagnose a swing issue.**"

> "Typically, **there is more than one error contributing** to the problems. To top it off,
> **everyone has bad shots from time to time**, which are outside their personal norms."

**W.H. Fuller**, *Small-bore Target Shooting* (1963), UK Historic Arms Resource Centre —
rifle, but the most rigorous treatment of the inference problem I found:

> "Although the reasons for inaccuracies may be causal, they have to be considered as
> **casual**, because there is little to be recognised to account for the departures from
> accuracy."

> "The diagram covers faults that are most likely to be made by the marksman, but **similar
> departures can be produced by faults in the rifle or in the ammunition or in both.**"

> "...elaborate records do not give any better diagnosis than rough guides." (on shot recording)

**The disagreement, stated plainly:** the Target Shooting Canada / Georgia 4-H chart presents
POI position → fault as a usable diagnostic. Patrick Henry (Aegis) says the chart is the most
misused tool in the industry and that patterns diagnose *trends, never individuals*. Fuller
says the same departures can come from rifle or ammunition, not the shooter, and that
magnitude is uninformative — *"A shooting fault that puts a shot just out of the ring is the
same fault that puts it into the 8-ring. The type of error has to be found, not its
magnitude."*

## 6. Evidence quality — what is actually measured

| Source | Type | Evidence for the mapping |
|---|---|---|
| Target Shooting Canada chart | Instructional handout, no author named, © 2003 | **None.** No data, no sample, no statistics. |
| Patrick Henry, Aegis Academy | Named instructor, industry training | **None** — and he argues *against* using it. |
| W.H. Fuller (1963) | Book, named author | Explicit statistical reasoning (laws of chance), no modern statistics. |
| Jones, Classé, Hester & Harris (1996) | **Peer-reviewed**, pilot study | **Yes** — see below. |

**The only peer-reviewed finding here is about dominance, not pattern position.** Jones,
Classé, Hester & Harris, "Association between eye dominance and training for rifle
marksmanship: a pilot study" (1996), 308 military recruits, Fort Benning:

> "The subjects with right-hand/right-eye and left-hand/left-eye (uncrossed) dominance had
> qualification scores that were **significantly higher (p = .009)** than the subjects with
> right hand/left-eye and left-hand/right-eye (crossed) dominance. A significantly higher
> percentage of subjects with uncrossed dominance achieved rifle qualification (**86.1
> percent**) than subjects with crossed dominance (**56.5 percent**) (p = .000)."

**What this does and does not license.** It establishes that crossed hand/eye dominance is
associated with **worse qualification scores** — a large, significant effect in a real sample.
It does **not** establish a POI offset direction, it is **rifle** not handgun, and it is a
*pilot* study the authors describe as such. I record it because it is the only hard number I
found, and specifically **not** as a pattern-position effect.
## 7. Patterns with MANY causes vs nearly unambiguous

**Many causes (do not diagnose):** high-centre, low-centre, left, right, low-left, low-right,
high-right, horizontal-centre, and scattered. **Every entry on the chart.** Each lists 2–3
unrelated mechanisms.

**Nearly unambiguous: none.** Not one pattern in the available literature maps to a single
fault with high confidence.

**The closest to unambiguous is canting**, and only because cant is *visible* — the target's
own ellipse shows it — rather than inferred. Fuller notes cant and hand position both produce
the same class of departure, so even this is not clean.

## 8. Group size is not skill

The brief asks this be stated if sources do. **No source I found says group size is a
markmanship deficit**, and two say the opposite in substance:

- Fuller: *"from averages of leading marksmen through the years, it is known that **the
  marksman is not as yet, the weakest link in the chain**"* — and separately that rifle and
  ammunition *"will produce groups of specific size and shape"*, with faults in one *"often
  corrected by recoil, jump and flip"*.
- Henry: a large group reflects *"more than one error contributing"*, and errors that are grip,
  trigger and stance rather than aim.

**So: large groups are usually mechanical or multi-fault, not a lack of marksmanship.** The
chart's own scattered-group entry says the same: *"All problems are evident, nothing specific
stands out."*
